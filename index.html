What you need to learn is how AI changes testing strategies, expected results, defect classification, and release decisions.

1. Traditional QA vs. AI QA

Consider a simple example.

Traditional application:

You enter a ZIP code, click Search, and expect the application to return matching businesses.

You can validate the result against known records.

AI application:

You ask an AI assistant:

“Find roofing companies in Los Angeles that might need help improving their online marketing.”

The AI might return different recommendations each time, even when the question is identical.

Now QA must determine:

  • Are the businesses real?
  • Are they actually located in Los Angeles?
  • Did the AI invent addresses or phone numbers?
  • Did it follow the user's requirements?
  • Are the recommendations relevant?
  • Does it expose information it shouldn't?
  • Does it behave consistently enough for production?

The fundamental difference: Traditional QA often checks for exact expected outputs. AI QA also measures the correctness, relevance, consistency, safety, and reliability of outputs that can legitimately vary.

2. Understand what you're testing in an AI application

Consider an AI assistant that answers insurance policy questions by reading company documents.

As QA, you're testing several layers, not just the final answer.

3. Six important AI testing concepts

ConceptWhat QA validates
Hallucination testingAI doesn't invent facts, prices, policy provisions, or other unsupported details
Groundedness testingAnswers are supported by the retrieved source documents
Prompt injection testingMalicious instructions inside user inputs or retrieved documents don't override trusted instructions
Consistency testingRepeated runs remain acceptably accurate even when wording varies
RAG testingRelevant documents are retrieved and the model uses them correctly
AI regression testingChanges to the model, prompt, or knowledge base don't introduce unacceptable failures

Prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, and misinformation are among the risks covered in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications.

4. Writing AI test cases

Let's test an insurance AI assistant that answers questions using policy documents.

Explore a sample AI QA test case

TEST INPUT

Can I add my son's vehicle to my existing auto policy?

EXPECTED BEHAVIOR

Use the applicable policy and eligibility rules. Ask for missing details and avoid confirming coverage or an effective date without an authoritative source.

FAILURE CONDITION

The assistant guarantees eligibility or immediate coverage without supporting policy information.

Illustrative test scenarios, not insurance advice.

Notice that we're specifying expected behavior, not necessarily an exact sentence.

5. What are AI Evals?

Evals is short for evaluations.

An evaluation measures how well an AI system performs against defined expectations.

You create an evaluation dataset containing test inputs, reference answers or expected behaviors, and scoring criteria. You then run the application against that dataset.

Some checks can be deterministic (for example, checking exact account IDs). Others need human review or a model-based grader to assess whether an answer is correct and supported.

For example, imagine we run 100 approved insurance questions against our AI assistant.

What should a QA lead investigate?

Review the 13 failures by severity and root cause. Check source retrieval, prompt instructions, generated claims, and authorization. A single critical disclosure can block release even when aggregate scores look strong.

A QA lead would investigate those 13 failures, categorize them, and determine their risk. A high pass rate does not justify releasing an application that discloses private information or invents consequential policy details.

6. How do you decide whether AI is ready for production?

You establish release criteria with business, engineering, and risk stakeholders.

For example, these are illustrative criteria, not universal industry thresholds:

  • All critical authorization and privacy tests must pass.
  • Zero known critical prompt-injection vulnerabilities.
  • At least 95% pass rate on a defined suite of routine business questions.
  • No unacceptable regression against the previously approved model.
  • Latency, availability, and API cost must stay within agreed limits.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework recommends measuring AI functionality, trustworthiness, uncertainty, and risk throughout the system lifecycle.
